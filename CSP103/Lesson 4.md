## Historical Development in Computer Architecture
### Overview of Key Miletones
- Background:
	- The evolution of computer architecture reflects a journey of continuous innovation and adaptation. 
	- From the early mechanical calculators to today's advanced microcomputers and mobile devices, each significant development has paved the way for the next technological leap. 
	- Understanding these milestones provides insight into how modern computing systems were shaped and how they function today.
1. The Pascaline and Stepped Reckoner
	- Pascaline (1642)
		- Inventor:
			- Blaise Pascal
		- Description:
			- The Pascaline was one of the earliest mechanical calculators, designed to assist Pascal's father with arithmetic calculations. 
			- It used a series of wheels and gears to perform addition and subtraction.
		- Mechanism:
			- The device featured dials and gears that could be manipulated to add or subtract numbers, representing the foundational concept of mechanizing arithmetic operations.
		- Significance:
			- This invention demonstrated the potential of mechanical devices to perform complex calculations, marking a pivotal step in the history of computing. 
			- It reduced human error and provided a new level of computational efficiency.
		- Figure:
			![[../.images/CSP103/CSP103-12.png]]
	- Stepped Reckoner (1672)
		- Inventor:
			- Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
		- Description:
			- The Stepped Reckoner improved upon Pascal’s design by enabling multiplication and division. 
			- It introduced the concept of a stepped drum mechanism, which was influential in the development of future mechanical calculators
		- Mechanism:
			- Leibniz’s machine used a series of rotating drums with varying steps to perform arithmetic operations, showcasing an advanced understanding of mechanical computation.
		- Significance: 
			- The Stepped Reckoner's ability to perform a broader range of calculations laid the groundwork for future developments in mechanical computing and inspired subsequent innovations in the field.
		- Figure:
			![[../.images/CSP103/CSP103-13.png]]
2. Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine
	- The Analytical Engine (1837)
		- Inventor:
			- Charles Babbage
		- Description:
			- The Analytical Engine was envisioned as a general-purpose mechanical computer, incorporating an arithmetic logic unit (ALU), control flow via loops and conditionals, and memory. 
			- Although it was never completed, its design was revolutionary.
		- Mechanism:
			- It featured punched cards for input, an ALU for calculations, and a separate memory for data storage. 
			- Its architecture was similar to modern computers, with programmability at its core.
		- Significance:
			- Babbage’s ideas were decades ahead of his time. 
			- The Analytical Engine is considered a precursor to the modern computer, introducing concepts such as programmability and the separation of data and instructions.
		- Figure:
			![[../.images/CSP103/CSP103-14.png]]
3. The Paradigm Shift: Mechanical to Electronic Computation
	- Electronic Numerical Integrator and Computer (ENIAC, 1945)
		- Description:
			- ENIAC was the first large-scale, general-purpose electronic digital computer designed to compute artillery firing tables for the U.S. Army. 
			- It used vacuum tubes instead of mechanical parts, allowing for significantly faster calculations.
		- Mechanism: 
			- ENIAC’s use of vacuum tubes marked a shift from mechanical to electronic components, enabling much higher speeds and more complex computations.
		- Significance: 
			- ENIAC represented a monumental leap in computing technology, demonstrating the potential of electronic digital computers to perform complex calculations and solve real-world problems more efficiently than mechanical predecessors.
4. The von Neumann Architecture and the Stored Program Concept
	- von Neumann Architecture (1945)
		- Inventor:
			- John von Neumann
		- Description:
			- The von Neumann architecture introduced the concept of storing program instructions in memory alongside data. 
			- This design allowed computers to switch between tasks without physical reconfiguration.
		- Mechanism:
			- It included a central processing unit (CPU) that performed computations and executed instructions, a memory unit for storing data and instructions, and input/output devices for interaction with the external world.
		- Significance:
			- The von Neumann architecture became the foundation for all modern computer designs. 
			- It enabled versatility in computing tasks and formed the basis for subsequent computer and software developments.
		- Figure:
			![[../.images/CSP103/CSP103-15.png]]
5. The Microcomputer Revolution
	- Microcomputers (1970s-1980s)
		- Description:
			- The development of the microprocessor in the 1970s, such as Intel’s 4004, led to the creation of microcomputers—small, affordable computers for personal use.
		- Examples:
			- Altair 8800 (1975) 
				- The first commercially successful personal computer, marking the beginning of the personal computing era.
			- Apple I (1976) 
				- Developed by Steve Jobs and Steve Wozniak, Apple’s first product introduced personal computing to a wider audience. 
			- IBM PC (1981)
				- Standardized personal computing and set the stage for modern PCs with its open architecture and wide adoption
		- Significance: 
			- The microcomputer revolution democratized computing, making it accessible to individuals and small businesses and laying the foundation for the personal computing industry.
6. The Impact of Mobile Devices on Personal Computing
	- Mobile Computing
		- Description:
			- The shift from traditional desktops to mobile devices like smartphones and tablets has transformed personal computing, integrating communication and computing functions into portable devices.
		- Evolution:
			- Early laptops paved the way for smartphones, which offer a range of functionalities from communication to complex computing tasks in a compact form.
		- Digital Transformation:
			- Mobile devices have revolutionized how users work, communicate, and access information, driving the rise of remote work, cloud computing, and the gig economy.
		- Significance:
			- Mobile devices have made computing more versatile and accessible, influencing how people interact with technology and each other.
7. Convergence on Computing Technologies: Supercomputers and Mobile Devices
	- Supercomputers and Mobile Devices
		- Description:
			- Both supercomputers, which solve complex scientific problems, and mobile devices, designed for personal use, utilize similar advanced technologies such as parallel processing and high-efficiency power management.
		- Technological Convergence:
			- Cloud Computing:
				- Allows mobile devices to offload tasks to remote servers, enhancing performance and capabilities.
			- Edge Computing:
				- Brings processing closer to the user, enabling realtime data analysis and applications.
		- Significance:
			- The convergence of technologies between supercomputers and mobile devices highlights the interconnected nature of modern computing systems and their collective advancements in efficiency and capability.
